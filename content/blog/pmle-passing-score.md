## Summary

Google does not publish a PMLE passing score on its certification page (checked October 16, 2026). Its official certification FAQ says Google Cloud exams provide **pass/fail results**, not numerical scores. Do not turn a practice-test percentage into an alleged official cutoff.

Last checked October 16, 2026.

Testero is independent and not affiliated with Google.

## What Google says about exam results

The [official certification FAQ](https://support.google.com/cloud-certification/answer/9438208?hl=en) explains that Google Cloud exams determine whether an individual meets a minimum passing standard. It says “only pass/fail results are provided” and explains that the exam is not designed to evaluate individuals on a scale of ability.

That establishes the result format. It does not give us a numerical PMLE cutoff. This article therefore does not offer a percentage, scaled-score target, or required number of correct answers. A precise-looking number without an official source would not make your booking decision better.

Google’s FAQ also says passing depends on correct answers across the entire exam: you do not need to “pass” each section separately. That is different from saying you should ignore weaker sections. Use the [PMLE exam domains and weights](/blog/pmle-exam-domains-weights) as a coverage checklist, not as a formula for reverse-engineering a secret cutoff.

## Sample-question results are not a prediction

Google’s [official PMLE sample questions](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSeYmkCANE81qSBqLW0g2X7RoskBX9yGYQu-m1TtsjMvHabGqg/viewform) carry an explicit warning: they do not represent the range of topics or level of difficulty on the exam. Google says performance on those questions should not be used to predict your exam result.

Use the samples to inspect question format and example content. After answering, explain why the selected option fits the scenario and why the alternatives do not. If you recognize the answer from an earlier attempt, treat that as familiarity with the item—not independent evidence that you can solve a new scenario.

The same distinction matters with practice material generally: a practice percentage describes performance on that material. It is not an official Google result or a guarantee about a different assessment.

## Better study signals than a made-up cutoff

The following are preparation suggestions, not Google scoring rules:

- **Explain the constraint.** Before choosing a product or architecture, name the scenario’s latency, cost, data, or operational requirement.
- **Compare alternatives.** Explain why a plausible competing approach is less suitable. Do not stop at recognizing a familiar service name.
- **Track errors by cause.** Separate a missing concept from a missed qualifier, a product-name mix-up, or a pacing problem. Each needs a different fix.
- **Check coverage.** Review every guide section and record which objectives you can explain and which need documentation or hands-on work.
- **Revisit with a new scenario.** Change a requirement and explain whether your original decision still holds.

A [domain-by-domain PMLE study plan](/blog/pmle-study-plan-by-domain) can turn those observations into the next study session. These signals help you choose what to work on; none predicts a pass.

## If you have already taken the exam

Google’s FAQ says unsuccessful candidates can access a detailed score report under “Exam History” in the Candidate Portal. It describes section-level feedback as guidance for prioritizing preparation, not an exact prediction of the next attempt.

Use that feedback alongside your own notes about unfamiliar topics. Do not interpret a section’s approximate exam weight as a personal numerical score. Return to the current guide and build a concrete review task for each weakness.
